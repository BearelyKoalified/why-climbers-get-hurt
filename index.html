Why climbers get hurt

Seven decades of American Alpine Club accident reports, sorted by how often each mistake actually shows up. The headline is not a surprise — climbers fall off rock. The useful part is the second column, where the club records what the climber did before the fall.

The tables answer two different questions

Table III of each annual report splits causation in two, and conflating the halves is the most common way the data gets misread. Almost every popular summary of this dataset quotes the first half and calls it “causes of climbing accidents,” which is how you end up with the finding that climbing accidents are caused by falling.

Immediate cause

The physical event that produced the injury: a fall on rock, a falling block, an avalanche, a rappel that came apart. Mostly a description of the accident, not an error.

Contributory cause

What the climber or the party did, or failed to do, that let the event happen: no protection placed, climbing unroped, out of their depth, no helmet. This is the mistakes column.

Counting rule

An accident can carry several tags in both halves, so the counts below are tags, not accidents, and the percentages are shares of all reported accidents rather than slices of a pie. Across the cumulative series there are 1.38 immediate-cause tags and 0.95 contributory tags per accident. Treat every number here as “appeared in this share of accident reports.”

The mistakes, ranked

Contributory causes, 1951–2016, United States and Canada combined. Three failures carry 3,581 of the tags between them: no rope, not enough skill for the ground, and no useful protection in the rock.

What the ranking says

  • Climbing unroped (1,284) and climbing alone (544) are the same story told twice. Together they carry 1,828 tags, and they often appear on the same report. Both skew old: both are mostly mid-century alpine and scrambling accidents, and both have nearly vanished from recent single-year counts.
  • Inexperience (1,263) is the one that never fades. It is the only top-three contributory cause still tagged at roughly its historical rate in recent data, and it is the only cause that appears near the top of both halves of the table — 640 times as the immediate cause as well.
  • Protection failure is bigger than its rank suggests. “Placed no or inadequate protection” (1,034) plus pulled nuts and cams (277) plus pulled pitons and ice screws (102) is 1,413 contributory tags, which puts the gear-in-the-rock family ahead of everything but the rope itself.
  • Belay and position errors sit mid-table but are rising. Inadequate belay (332) covers miscommunication, ineffective belay and no belay at all; poor position (281) is the belayer standing too far from the wall or in the fall line.

What actually happened

Immediate causes, same period. A fall or slip on rock is the immediate cause in 4,489 accidents — more than half of everything in the series, and more than the next six categories combined.

Reading the long tail

The interesting entries are the ones whose rank badly understates their current importance. Rappel failure (469) sits seventh over the whole series but is the clearest growth category in the data. Lowering error (7) looks trivial only because the club did not track it separately until the 2016 edition; in 2017 alone it was tagged 12 times. Meanwhile crampon errors, glissading accidents and exposure — the signature causes of the mountaineering era — have gone quiet.

How the club defines the descent errors

Rappel failure / error covers inadequate anchors, uneven ropes, no knots in the rope ends, simul-rappelling errors, pendulum swings and attaching the device incorrectly. Lowering error, added as its own category in 2016, covers a rope that was too short, miscommunication, and lowering with the wrong rope. Before 2016 lowering accidents were folded into the rappel line, so the rappel total is slightly inflated for earlier years and the lowering total is close to meaningless.

How these events actually happened

Table III stops at the top level: it will tell you 469 accidents began with a rappel failure, but not whether the anchor pulled, the ropes were uneven, or the climber came off the ends. Two sources go a level deeper. The club itself published a sub-cause breakdown for rappelling, and every published report carries an editor-written headline of the form RAPPEL ERROR | Uneven Ropes, No Stopper Knots, which can be counted.

How rappels fail, in the club's own numbers

This is the one top-level cause the AAC has broken down itself, covering every rappelling accident reported over twelve years. Anchors and backups are the headline, but the largest single slice is the simplest mistake on the list: two rope ends that were not the same length.

Three of these six are the same thirty seconds

Uneven ropes (29%), no rappel backup (19.4%) and poor technique (11.3%) all describe the same moment at the anchor, before weight goes on the system. Together they are 60% of rappelling accidents, and the checks that catch them — pulling the ends level, tying stopper knots, rigging an autoblock — cost well under a minute.

Mechanisms by event type

Counted from the headline tags of all 61 accident reports published in the 2018 edition. All four panels share one scale, so bars are comparable across panels. These are mechanisms only; the factors describing the climber rather than the event are separated out below.

Each panel has a different character. Rappelling and lowering failures are almost entirely rigging errors — the rope was the wrong length, the ends were uneven, there was no knot and no backup. Roped falls on rock are dominated by communication: seven of 25 reports turned on one climber believing they were on belay when they were not, which is more than protection failures of every kind combined. Snow and ice comes down to arrest skills. Stranding is almost purely route-finding.

What the climber brought to it

The same reports also carry tags that describe the party or the day rather than the mechanism. These are the conditions under which the mechanisms above get a chance to operate.

Inexperience leads, and it cuts across every panel. No helmet is tagged in 7 of 61 reports and in a third of the rappelling and lowering group, where the usual pattern is a short uncontrolled fall onto rock — the exact case a helmet is for.

The mix has shifted under the cumulative totals

The cumulative column is dominated by decades when the reports were mostly about mountaineering. Comparing each cause's share of the whole series against its share of one recent year shows which entries are historical and which are live. The 2017 column is 186 accidents, so this is a direction, not a measurement.

The modern profile

Rising: everything to do with the transitions at the top and bottom of a pitch — rappelling, lowering, belaying, finding the anchor, trusting a hold or a cam placement. Fading: everything to do with being alone, unroped and far from the road. That is what you would expect from a sport whose centre of mass moved from alpine routes to bolted and trad single pitches, and it means the historical ranking is a poor guide to what is likely to hurt a climber today.

What independent analysis of the narratives adds

The club's tables pick causes category by category but do not cross-tabulate, so they cannot say which discipline a mistake belongs to. An investigation by Climbing re-tagged the accident narratives themselves to get at that.

  • 2,770 narratives from 1990 onward were re-coded, about 56% of the accidents the club logged for the period — only published write-ups could be tagged.
  • Trad accounted for 63% of roped rock accidents against 20% for sport and toprope combined, though the gap has narrowed from roughly 5× in 1990–2009 to about 2× since.
  • Inadequate or pulled protection was tagged in 49% of trad incidents — the single most frequent factor for any style in that dataset, which matches where the club's own protection family lands.
  • About 32% of incidents happened on descent, and rappel error was the leading descent factor at 29%; no backup or end knot appeared in 12%.
  • Rappel error was tagged in 251 incidents, 76 of them fatal — close to a 30% fatality rate, which makes it the deadliest of the common factors rather than merely a frequent one.
  • On toprope, anchor failure led; in sport, miscommunication appeared in roughly 8%.

The rappel trend has not turned around

The club published 14 rappel accidents in 2023, 8 of them fatal, and the 2025 edition reports 15 rappel incidents involving 23 climbers with 5 deaths. Anchor material is a recurring thread: webbing recovered from a fatal 2022 rappel-anchor failure at Tahquitz Rock broke at 2.54 kN, 3.56 kN and 3.2 kN in three pull tests, against the 22 kN a new sling is rated for.

What this data cannot tell you

  • There is no denominator. The tables count accidents, not accidents per climbing day. Nothing here says trad is more dangerous than sport, or that September is risky; it says more reports arrive from those conditions. Participation estimates exist for recent years only.
  • Reporting is voluntary and filtered twice. Not every accident is reported, and not every report is published. Gym accidents, bouldering and overuse injuries are badly under-represented, and the club notes experienced climbers file reports more readily — which is the likeliest reason “experienced” outnumbers “none or little” in the experience table.
  • The cumulative column is not a modern sample. It is a 66-year aggregate, weighted toward an era with different routes, gear and norms. For current risk, use recent single-year columns and accept the small-sample noise.
  • Category definitions moved. Lowering error arrived in 2016; the “top or bottom of route” phase category arrived in 2001. The club also notes that the 1986 and 1997 editions repeated some prior-year data, corrected later in the cumulative figures.
  • Some published cumulative rows do not survive inspection. The age distribution in the 2018 edition reports 1,249 accidents to climbers under 15 but only 436 to climbers over 50, and gives Canada 18 accidents in the 31–35 band against 145 in 36–50. Those rows look like accumulated transcription errors; the cause tables, which cross-check cleanly against earlier editions, do not share the problem. I have reproduced the cause tables only.
