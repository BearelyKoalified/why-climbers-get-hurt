Contributing factors, ranked

All years, United States and Canada combined. No rope, not enough skill for the ground, and no useful protection account for 3,581 of the tags between them.

Immediate causes, ranked

A fall or slip on rock is the immediate cause in 4,489 accidents — more than half the series, and more than the next six categories combined.

How rappels fail

The one category the club has broken down itself. Uneven ropes, no backup and poor technique are 60% of rappelling accidents, and all three are the same thirty seconds at the anchor.

Mechanisms by event type

From the headline tags of the published 2017 reports. All four panels share one scale. Rappelling fails on rigging; roped falls on rock fail on communication.

Background factors

All years against 2017

Each cause as a share of accidents in its period. One year is a small sample, so read the direction, not the multiple. Rappelling, lowering and route-finding are up; unroped and alone are nearly gone.

Before you quote a number

  • No denominator. These are accident counts, not accidents per climbing day. Nothing here shows one discipline is more dangerous than another.
  • Accidents carry several tags — 1.38 immediate causes and 0.95 contributory causes each — so shares never sum to 100%.
  • The cumulative columns are a 66-year aggregate weighted toward the mountaineering era, and the mix has shifted hard toward rappelling, lowering and belay errors.
  • Two blocks are reproduced but should not be quoted. The published age bands and the U.S. September figure are internally inconsistent; each is flagged where it appears.
